# Source and priority assessment

Prepared 2026-09-10. This is a bounded source review, not an exhaustive search
or a certification that the question remained open worldwide until this work.

## Primary problem and known positive direction

Samuel A. Alexander, *Biologically unavoidable sequences*, Electronic Journal
of Combinatorics 20(1), P31 (2013), DOI [10.37236/3035](https://doi.org/10.37236/3035).
The [published PDF](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/v20i1p31/pdf/)
was inspected for Definition 1, Proposition 9, Theorem 10 and the questions
in Section 6, page 12. The nine-page [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.0186)
was also read. Its theorem numbering differs. The arXiv record begins in
December 2012; the published paper is dated 5 February 2013. The proposed
submission uses 2013 and explicitly points to the published question.

The published final question asks for the classification and whether any
non-eventually-periodic sequence is unavoidable. Theorem 10 already proves
the positive direction. Our novel-result claim, if priority survives checking,
is the explicit avoiding construction for every remaining sequence.

Alexander's *Infinite graphs in systematic biology, with an application to
the species problem* also states the biological classification question.
It was located as a related primary source, not treated as a later solution.

## Searches and their limits

During September 9–10 the research used exact-title searches and combinations
of “biologically unavoidable”, “eventually periodic”, “classification” and
“Thue–Morse”. The retrieved relevant results included the original paper,
arXiv/publisher copies and the related Alexander work. No subsequent
classification proof was verified in those searches.

The search engine also returned unrelated biological sequence material; those
results were not treated as mathematical evidence. We have not completed an
exhaustive forward-citation search, checked all subsequent publications by
the author, or received an author/expert response. Missed prior work remains
possible.

König's lemma, eventual-period arguments, and halting-problem reductions are
standard tools. The paper does not claim them as new. Degree-growth
universality arguments have antecedents in the locally finite graph
literature; no such argument is the headline result of this submission.
